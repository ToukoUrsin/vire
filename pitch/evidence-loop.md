# The closed loop in the AirPods: the evidence

Every source below was opened on 14 Nov 2026: the full text where it is open, the abstract where it is not, and each entry says which. Local copies, search outputs and the scripts that fetched them: `/bulk2/runs/depression/evidence-loop/` (`sources/`, `dl/`). Format and rules follow `claims.md` and `evidence-airpods.md`; nothing here contradicts rows 29, 30, 32, 33, 75 or 76.

Rule, as before: these papers are about groups of people, programmes of several weeks, research earbuds and other devices. None of them tested Vire, AirPods breathing, or people after TMS. Nothing here is our result.

## What we may say, and what we must not (6 lines)

1. **May:** "Breathing slowly, around six breaths a minute, makes the heart rate swing more with each breath while you do it. That is one of the best measured effects in this field." (Laborde 2022: 223 studies; Lehrer and Gevirtz 2014.)
2. **May:** "As a programme of several weeks, breathing with heart-rate feedback lowered depressive symptoms by a small to medium amount in randomised trials, g about 0.4, mostly in people whose main problem was something else, like heart disease or stress." (Pizzoli 2021; Vann-Adibe 2026; Fincham 2023 for breathing exercises in general.)
3. **May:** "Meru Health put this breathing, about six a minute with a heart-rate trace, into an eight-week app programme with a therapist. In a comparison that was not randomised, more people improved with it, 56% against 29%. The whole programme beat a waiting list in a randomised trial. Neither study shows what the breathing did on its own."
4. **May:** "Tones that follow your breath can lead people into slow breathing, and earbud motion can read breathing rate to within about two to three breaths a minute when people keep still. Neither has been tested on AirPods, and the earbud readings were tested on normal breathing, not on breathing at six a minute."
5. **May:** "Learning which session suits a person is a research method, micro-randomised trials. It is not a proven benefit: the one trial we found that set learned choice against random choice saw a small gain in one of three outcomes."
6. **Must not:** brain waves or EEG from AirPods (they have no such sensor); "a session is a treatment"; "one session lowers depression" (or that Vire lowers depression at all); HRV or beat-to-beat timing from AirPods (Apple documents heart rate in bpm, during workouts, iOS 26 and later); "music therapy" for our tones; "personalisation works"; "Meru proved biofeedback works".

## 1. HRV biofeedback and depressive symptoms

**Pizzoli, Marzorati, Gatti, Monzani, Mazzocco, Pravettoni 2021.** "A meta-analysis on heart rate variability biofeedback and depressive symptoms." Sci Rep 11:6650. doi:10.1038/s41598-021-86149-7 (full text, PMC7988005). Same paper as `claims.md` row 32.
- Type and n: meta-analysis of 14 RCTs, 794 adults.
- Finding: "The random effect analysis yielded a medium mean effect size g = 0.38 [95% CI = 0.16, 0.60; 95% PI = -0.19, 0.96]", I² = 45%. Egger's test not significant; trim-and-fill added no studies.
- In whom: "Nine studies were conducted on patients with cardiovascular disease, stress symptoms, cancer or alcohol disorder, while the remaining five studies focused on non-clinical samples."
- Compared with what: effect sizes at the end of the programme, HRVB against "control conditions or other active treatments".
- Limits: few studies were in people with depression as their main diagnosis; the prediction interval crosses zero, so a new study could find no effect; the authors say it is unclear "which biofeedback protocol and devices yield the best results".

**Vann-Adibe, Tsui, Zhou, Ciren, Li, Cha 2026.** "Efficacy and Methodology of Remote Heart Rate Variability Biofeedback Interventions for Mental Health: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis." Appl Psychophysiol Biofeedback 51(3):441-452. doi:10.1007/s10484-025-09750-w (full text, PMC13525029). The newest meta-analysis we found.
- Type and n: systematic review and meta-analysis of remote (home) HRVB, 18 studies, 1,352 people; depression pooled from 10 studies.
- Finding: depression g = -0.41 (95% CI -0.05 to -0.77; printed in the paper as "[- 0.049, - 0.772]"), I² = 73%, k = 10, "compared to control conditions". HRV rose too (g = 0.44, k = 10). In people with a psychological condition, depression g = -0.58 (CI -0.19 to -0.97, k = 6). Stress did not change significantly (k = 8).
- What predicted more effect: "maximizing resonance, the screen being on the device, and less than 20 min of daily practice time were beneficial for improving HRV outcomes". None of the moderators was significant for depression.
- Limits: includes non-randomised studies (the effect held when they were left out); high heterogeneity; the wide interval reaches close to zero; the "under 20 minutes a day" finding is about HRV, not mood.

**Lehrer, Kaur, Sharma et al. 2020.** "Heart Rate Variability Biofeedback Improves Emotional and Physical Health and Performance: A Systematic Review and Meta Analysis." Appl Psychophysiol Biofeedback 45(3):109-129. doi:10.1007/s10484-020-09466-z (abstract only).
- Type and n: meta-analysis of 58 randomised controlled studies across all outcomes.
- Finding: "a significant small to moderate effect size was found favoring HRVB"; "HRVB has the largest effect sizes for anxiety, depression, anger and athletic/artistic performance"; "Effect sizes are larger in comparison to inactive than active control conditions although significant for both."
- Limits: the abstract gives no depression figure, so we quote none. "With a small number of studies for each" outcome. Lehrer co-developed the method.

**Donnelly, Georgiadis, Stavrou 2023**, Acta Biomed 94(4):e2023214, doi:10.23750/abm.v94i4.14305 (abstract): 9 studies, 428 adults with other illnesses, depression g = 0.478 (CI 0.212 to 0.743) against standard treatment. Same direction; smaller and in other illnesses. Not needed on stage.

**What it adds up to.** As a practised skill over weeks, HRVB lowers depressive symptoms a little, about as much as other add-on treatments. The trials are mostly small, mostly not in primary depression, and none after TMS.

## 2. Meru Health: what the programme is, and what each study was

**Economides, Lehrer, Ranta, Nazander, Hilgert, Raevuori, Gevirtz, Khazan, Forman-Hoffman 2020.** "Feasibility and Efficacy of the Addition of Heart Rate Variability Biofeedback to a Remote Digital Health Intervention for Depression." Appl Psychophysiol Biofeedback 45(2):75-86. doi:10.1007/s10484-020-09458-z (full text, PMC7250954).
- **Not an RCT.** "We used a quasi-experimental, non-equivalent (matched) groups design to compare changes in symptoms of depression in the enhanced group (n = 48) to historical outcome data from the standard group (n = 48)." The authors: "participants were not randomly allocated, the researchers were not blinded to group, and we did not include a conventional control group."
- The programme (both groups): 8 weekly modules "derived from evidence-based practices such as Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction (MBSR), Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy (MBCT), Cognitive-Behavioral Therapy (CBT), and Behavioral Activation Therapy (BAT)", audio-guided mindfulness, a licensed therapist by message, and an anonymous peer group.
- The breathing (enhanced group only): a HeartMath Bluetooth pulse sensor on the earlobe or fingertip; "a visual pacer that expands during inhalation for 4 s and contracts during exhalation for 6 s, achieving resonance breathing at rate of approximately 6 breaths per minute", with a live heart-rate trace that turns green at "high resonance". Visual, not sound. Average 3.86 hours over 25.8 sessions in 8 weeks; sessions grew from about 6 to 11 minutes.
- People: PHQ-9 of 10 or more (mean 15.3), 81% women, 91% in Finland, 34% on antidepressants.
- Finding: response (PHQ-9 down by half or more and under 10) in 56.3% against 29.2%; OR 3.12 (95% CI 1.34 to 7.26); adjusted OR 3.44 (1.28 to 9.26); NNT 4 (2 to 12). More HRV-B hours went with larger PHQ-9 drops (b = 0.68, p = 0.037), but "HRV-B engagement did not predict the likelihood of clinically significant improvement" as a yes/no outcome (b = 0.2, p = 0.16).
- Limits: historical controls; the enhanced group also did more CBT/BAT exercises; nobody checked whether people actually breathed at resonance or whether their HRV rose; most authors worked for Meru Health.

**Forman-Hoffman, Sihvonen, Wielgosz, Kuhn, Nelson, Peiper, Gould 2024.** "Therapist-supported digital mental health intervention for depressive symptoms: A randomized clinical trial." J Affect Disord 349:494-501. doi:10.1016/j.jad.2024.01.057 (abstract; NCT04738084).
- **This one is an RCT**, of the whole programme: "a newer version of the therapist-supported and guided DMHI, the Meru Health Program (MHP), which was recently enhanced with heart rate variability biofeedback and lengthened from 8- to 12-weeks duration".
- n: 100 adults with elevated depressive symptoms; 54 programme, 46 waitlist, 12 weeks.
- Finding: greater fall in depression than waitlist (d = -0.8); minimal clinically important improvement in 39.1% against 9.8% (χ² = 9.90, p = .002).
- Limits: a waitlist control, so it cannot separate the breathing from the therapist, the CBT or the attention; four of seven authors list Meru Health.

**Economides, Ranta, Nazander et al. 2019.** "Long-Term Outcomes of a Therapist-Supported, Smartphone-Based Intervention for Elevated Symptoms of Depression and Anxiety: Quasiexperimental, Pre-Postintervention Study." JMIR Mhealth Uhealth 7(8):e14284. doi:10.2196/14284 (abstract). The 2020 paper cites it as the standard Meru programme (called Ascend here).
- **Not an RCT**: one group, before and after, 102 adults, no HRV biofeedback yet.
- Finding: PHQ-9 down 6.67 points (95% CI 5.59 to 7.75), held at 12 months.
- Limits: no control group; Meru authors.

**What we may say.** Meru put six-a-minute breathing with a heart trace inside a therapist-led CBT and mindfulness programme. The only randomised trial tested the whole package against a waiting list. The breathing on its own has only the non-randomised comparison.

## 3. Slow paced breathing around six a minute

**Lehrer and Gevirtz 2014.** "Heart rate variability biofeedback: how and why does it work?" Front Psychol 5:756. doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2014.00756 (full text, PMC4104929). Review.
- "During HRV biofeedback, the amplitude of heart rate oscillations grows to many times the amplitude at rest, while the pattern becomes simple and sinusoidal."
- "most studies find that maximum effects usually are achieved when breathing at a rate of approximately 0.1 Hz (six breaths per minute)"; later refined to "about 0.09 Hz, or 5.5 breaths per minute"; "This frequency varied from individual to individual."
- Most people normally breathe at "9 to 24 breaths per minute".
- Limits: a narrative review by the method's developers; mechanism (baroreflex) is the authors' leading account.

**Vaschillo, Vaschillo, Lehrer 2006.** "Characteristics of resonance in heart rate variability stimulated by biofeedback." Appl Psychophysiol Biofeedback 31(2):129-142. doi:10.1007/s10484-006-9009-3 (abstract).
- n: 32 adults with asthma and 24 healthy adults.
- Finding: each person has their own resonant frequency; "a negative relationship between resonant frequency and height, and a lower resonant frequency in men than women"; it "remains constant across 10 sessions".
- Limits: small, older lab work. It is the reason "about six" and not "exactly six": the best pace differs between people (relevant to section 7).

**Laborde, Allen, Borges et al. 2022.** "Effects of voluntary slow breathing on heart rate and heart rate variability: A systematic review and a meta-analysis." Neurosci Biobehav Rev 138:104711. doi:10.1016/j.neubiorev.2022.104711 (abstract only; the full text and the author copies were blocked to us).
- n: 223 studies: 172 measured during the breathing, 16 right after one session, 49 after a multi-session programme.
- Finding: "Results indicate increases in vmHRV with VSB, DURING, IM-AFTER1, and AFTER-INT" (vagally mediated HRV rose during, right after one session, and after a programme).
- Limits: it is about HRV, not mood. We could not open the effect sizes, so we quote none.

**Zaccaro, Piarulli, Laurino et al. 2018.** "How Breath-Control Can Change Your Life: A Systematic Review on Psycho-Physiological Correlates of Slow Breathing." Front Hum Neurosci 12:353. doi:10.3389/fnhum.2018.00353 (abstract; full text PMC6137615).
- n: 15 studies of 2,461 screened, healthy people.
- Finding: slow breathing goes with more HRV and respiratory sinus arrhythmia and with self-reported "increased comfort, relaxation, pleasantness, vigor and alertness, and reduced symptoms of arousal, anxiety, depression, anger, and confusion". Its EEG findings come from EEG caps in a lab.
- Limits: narrative synthesis, 15 small studies, healthy people. Not a reason to mention brain waves: AirPods have no EEG.

**Fincham, Strauss, Montero-Marin, Cavanagh 2023.** "Effect of breathwork on stress and mental health: A meta-analysis of randomised-controlled trials." Sci Rep 13:432. doi:10.1038/s41598-022-27247-y (full text, PMC9828383).
- Type and n: meta-analysis of RCTs; stress 12 trials, 785 adults; depressive symptoms 18 trials.
- Finding: stress g = -0.35 (CI -0.55 to -0.14); depressive symptoms "g = -0.40 [95% CI -0.58, -0.22] ... heterogeneity was moderate and significant", I² = 58%; anxiety g = -0.32.
- Programme length: "The minimum total estimated durations of an intervention/home practice ranged from 80 to 5625 min." No significant link between dose and effect.
- Limits: "Most studies were deemed as being at moderate risk of bias"; mixes slow and fast techniques; only one stress trial was in a mental health sample; the authors "urge caution ... to avoid a miscalibration between hype and evidence".

**Balban, Neri, Kogon et al. 2023.** "Brief structured respiration practices enhance mood and reduce physiological arousal." Cell Rep Med 4(1):100895. doi:10.1016/j.xcrm.2022.100895 (full text, PMC9873947).
- Type and n: remote randomised study, 108 adults, mostly from a Stanford undergraduate class; 5 minutes a day for a month; three breathing exercises against mindfulness meditation.
- Finding: breathing, "especially the exhale-focused cyclic sighing, produces greater improvement in mood (p < 0.05) and reduction in respiratory rate (p < 0.05) compared with mindfulness meditation." Average daily gain in positive affect 1.91 (breathing groups) against 1.22 (mindfulness).
- Limits: healthy students, "not pre-registered as a clinical trial", positive affect not depression, a month of practice.

**What one short session does and does not show.**
- Does: during a session of slow breathing, heart-rate swing with the breath grows (Lehrer and Gevirtz 2014; Laborde 2022), and vagal HRV is still up right after one session (Laborde 2022, 16 studies). That is a body effect we can measure while it happens.
- Does not: show that a 2 to 3 minute session lowers depressive symptoms. Every mood result above is from programmes of weeks. The closest test of a very short session (section 7, Schwerdtfeger 2025) found that one minute of breathing lowered stress no more than one minute of looking at an object.

## 4. Breathing rate from an earbud motion sensor

**Röddiger, Wolffram, Laubenstein, Budde, Beigl 2019.** "Towards Respiration Rate Monitoring Using an In-Ear Headphone Inertial Measurement Unit." EarComp 2019, pp. 48-53. doi:10.1145/3345615.3361130 (full PDF, local copy from round 5). Same paper as `claims.md` row 76.
- n: 12 people, eSense earbud at 50 Hz, nasal cannula pressure as reference, 20-second windows.
- Finding: mean absolute error 2.62 breaths a minute (accelerometer), 2.55 (gyroscope). By posture (accelerometer/gyroscope MAE): standing 3.15/2.45, sitting 3.10/2.74, supine 2.56/2.68; "we can not report significant differences between the two modalities or the three postures". "However, in general, performance varies drastically between participants."
- Posture: standing, sitting and lying on the back, "keeping as still as possible", breathing normally, some after jumping jacks.
- Limits: no walking or talking tested; normal breathing only; its filter starts at 0.1 Hz, "equivalent to 6 to 30 breath cycles per minute", so six a minute sits at the very edge of what it looks for.

**Ahmed, Rahman, Nemati, Ahmed, Kuang, Gao 2023 (Samsung Research America).** "Remote Breathing Rate Tracking in Stationary Position Using the Motion and Acoustic Sensors of Earables." CHI 2023. doi:10.1145/3544548.3581265 (abstract only, via Semantic Scholar; the ACM full text was blocked to us).
- n: "A user study with 30 participants".
- Finding: motion sensors plus microphones together give "Mean Absolute Error < 2 breaths per minute while retaining most breathing sessions (75%) performed in real-world settings".
- Where it fails: "small non-breathing head movements or background noises during the assessment affect the estimation accuracy"; filtering them out "can discard valid measurements". The title says stationary.
- Limits: we could not open the per-sensor or per-posture numbers; it uses the microphone as well as motion; Samsung earbuds, not AirPods.

**Liu, Butkow, Stuchbury-Wass, Pullin, Ma, Mascolo 2024 (RespEar).** "RespEar: Earable-Based Robust Respiratory Rate Monitoring." arXiv:2407.06901 (full PDF). Cited here for what it says about motion sensors, not for its own microphone method.
- Finding about motion sensors: methods "that use IMUs in ... earbuds ... require the user to remain still"; "When walking or running, the IMU clearly detects steps, but breathing signals are indiscernible, since walking and running generate strong motions that completely overshadow the breathing signal." On talking: "Breathing adapts to speaking", so they skip estimation while speaking.
- n: 18 people, 8 activities (their in-ear microphone method: MAE 1.48 sitting, 2.28 active).
- Limits: preprint; custom earbuds with in-ear microphones.

**Khan, Creswell, Albert et al. 2025.** "Mindfulness Meditation and Respiration: Accelerometer-Based Respiration Rate and Mindfulness Progress Estimation..." Proc ACM IMWUT 9(3) art. 95, doi:10.1145/3749498 (full text, arXiv:2507.17688). A phone on a chest strap, not an earbud; cited for one finding.
- Finding: "existing methods are inadequate for detecting the slow breathing rates (<10 bpm) characteristic of mindfulness practices" and "algorithms developed using natural breathing rate fail to detect the low breathing rate". Their own phone-on-chest method: MAE 1.6 breaths a minute; 40 people, 261 sessions.
- Limits: chest, not ear; Creswell and three co-authors are with the company that makes the app.

**What we may say.** Earbud motion has read breathing rate to within about 2 to 2.6 breaths a minute in people sitting, standing or lying still. Not while walking or talking. Not yet shown on AirPods (our Europe PMC and arXiv searches found no AirPods breathing study), and not checked at six breaths a minute, where methods built for normal breathing can fail. For a paced session we know the target rate, so the honest check is "did the breathing follow the pacer", and that we must test ourselves against a reference.

## 5. Sound that adapts to the person, and music for depression

**Elliot, Izzo, White, Rosing, Snyder, Alter, Gavish, Black 2004.** "Graded blood pressure reduction in hypertensive outpatients associated with use of a device to assist with slow breathing." J Clin Hypertens 6(10):553-559. doi:10.1111/j.1524-6175.2004.03553.x (full text, PMC8109322, read through the PMC page). The closest published thing to "sound that follows your breath".
- How the device works: a belt sensor and headphones; it "monitors and analyzes the respiratory patterns in real time", "synthesizes a personalized melody comprised of two distinct tones—one for inhalation and one for exhalation", and "gradually prolongs the exhalation tone, guiding the user to a slower respiratory rate (typically <10 BPM)".
- n: 149 people with high blood pressure randomised; breathing data from the device group.
- Finding on breathing: "95% of those who operated the device at least once achieved slow breathing using the instructions provided"; users "spent 45% of each session taking <10 BPM".
- Limits: blood pressure trial, not mood; a device maker's inventor (Gavish) is a co-author; time below 10 a minute, not a measured rate.

**Gavish 2010.** "Device-guided breathing in the home setting: technology, performance and clinical outcomes." Biol Psychol 84(1):150-156. doi:10.1016/j.biopsycho.2010.02.013 (abstract).
- Finding: the guiding tones are "generated in response to the monitored breathing pattern"; "Breathing modification was highly individualized, repeatable over sessions"; quality of following the tones "displayed a relationship with the clinical outcomes".
- Limits: inventor's own analysis; no n in the abstract; 8 weeks of 15-minute daily sessions.

**Mahtani, Nunan, Heneghan 2012**, J Hypertens 30(5):852-860, doi:10.1097/hjh.0b013e3283520077 (abstract), and **Landman et al. 2014**, JAMA Intern Med 174(11):1815-1821, doi:10.1001/jamainternmed.2014.4336 (abstract). The warning that goes with the device: 8 trials, 494 patients, systolic pressure down 3.67 mmHg, but "excluding the five trials sponsored by or involving the manufacturers of the device, ... no overall effect"; in blinded trials with an active control (music or a sham device), no significant drop.

**Bernardi, Porta, Sleight 2006.** "Cardiovascular, cerebrovascular, and respiratory changes induced by different types of music in musicians and non-musicians: the importance of silence." Heart 92(4):445-452. doi:10.1136/hrt.2005.064600 (abstract).
- n: 12 musicians, 12 controls, six music styles in random order.
- Finding: "Ventilation, blood pressure, and heart rate increased ... with faster tempi"; "The pause reduced heart rate, blood pressure, and minute ventilation, even below baseline"; "Musicians had greater respiratory sensitivity to the music tempo than did non-musicians."
- Limits: 24 people, minutes of listening; shows tempo moves breathing and arousal, not that music paces breathing to a target.

**Aalbers, Fusar-Poli, Freeman et al. 2017 (Cochrane).** "Music therapy for depression." Cochrane Database Syst Rev 11:CD004517. doi:10.1002/14651858.CD004517.pub3 (abstract).
- Type and n: 9 studies, 421 people with depression.
- Finding: music therapy added to usual care against usual care alone: clinician-rated SMD -0.98 (CI -1.69 to -0.27, 3 RCTs and 1 controlled trial, n = 219); self-rated SMD -0.85 (CI -1.37 to -0.34, n = 142), "moderate-quality evidence"; short-term.
- Limits: this is music therapy, with a therapist, active (singing, playing) or receptive; small trials; short term only. It is not evidence for background tones in an app.

**Lee, Kim, Yoon, Lee 2025.** "Music therapy for patients with depression: systematic review and meta-analysis of randomised controlled trials." BJPsych Open 11(5):e201. doi:10.1192/bjo.2025.10822 (abstract). SMD -0.97 (CI -1.23 to -0.71), but "The evidence level was very low due to high risk of bias, inconsistency due to high heterogeneity and imprecision."

**What we may say.** Tones that follow a person's breath and stretch the out-breath can lead most people into slow breathing; that was shown with a medical device in blood-pressure trials. Music therapy helps depression in small trials, but that is therapy with a therapist, not our tones.

## 6. Brief mindfulness, body scan, walking

**Kuyken, Warren, Taylor et al. 2016.** "Efficacy of Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy in Prevention of Depressive Relapse: An Individual Patient Data Meta-analysis From Randomized Trials." JAMA Psychiatry 73(6):565-574. doi:10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2016.0076 (abstract and full text, PMC6640038).
- Type and n: individual-patient meta-analysis, 9 RCTs, 1,258 people with recurrent depression in full or partial remission.
- Finding: relapse within 60 weeks, hazard ratio 0.69 (95% CI 0.58 to 0.82) against no MBCT, 0.79 (0.64 to 0.97) against active treatments including antidepressants. 229 of 596 (38%) relapsed with MBCT, 327 of 662 (49%) without.
- Limits: a full manualised MBCT course, not a short exercise; "the constituent trials were largely conducted by proponents of MBCT"; "There is a reduction in protective effects over time."

**Schumer, Lindsay, Creswell 2018.** "Brief mindfulness training for negative affectivity: A systematic review and meta-analysis." J Consult Clin Psychol 86(7):569-583. doi:10.1037/ccp0000324 (full text, PMC6441958, read through the PMC page).
- Type and n: 65 RCTs, 5,489 people, "from single-session inductions to multisession interventions lasting up to 2 weeks", mostly young adults new to meditation.
- Finding: g = .21 (95% CI .12 to .29) on negative affectivity (depression, rumination, anxiety, stress pooled). Single sessions g = .22, longer g = .12, no significant difference.
- **The catch:** publication bias was found, and after trim-and-fill the effect was "Hedges' g = .04 [-.01, .09]", not different from zero. No separate figure for depression.
- Limits: mostly students; mood right after, not symptoms over time.

**Body scan.** We did not find a meta-analysis of the body scan on its own for depression. It is part of MBCT, so we may say "a body scan, one of the practices in MBCT", and nothing about its own effect.

**Noetel, Sanders, Gallardo-Gómez et al. 2024.** "Effect of exercise for depression: systematic review and network meta-analysis of randomised controlled trials." BMJ 384:e075847. doi:10.1136/bmj-2023-075847 (abstract). Same paper as `claims.md` row 33.
- Type and n: 218 RCTs, 14,170 people meeting clinical cut-offs for major depression.
- Finding: walking or jogging Hedges' g -0.62 (95% credible interval -0.80 to -0.45; n = 1,210, 51 arms) against active controls; "effects of exercise were proportional to the intensity prescribed".
- Limits: exercise programmes over weeks; "only one study met the Cochrane criteria for low risk of bias"; confidence "low for walking or jogging".

**Weinstein, van Aert, Donovan, Muskens, Kop 2024.** "Affective Responses to Acute Exercise: A Meta-Analysis of the Potential Beneficial Effects of a Single Bout of Exercise on General Mood, Anxiety, and Depressive Symptoms." Psychosom Med 86(6):486-497. doi:10.1097/PSY.0000000000001321 (abstract).
- n: 103 studies, 4,671 people; mood measured before and within 30 minutes after one bout.
- Finding: depressive symptoms g = 0.407 (95% CI 0.249 to 0.564); general mood g = 0.336.
- Limits: before-after within the same people, not against a control; "substantial and statistically significant heterogeneity"; a bout of exercise, not a walk prompt.

**Meyer, Koltyn, Stegner, Kim, Cook 2016.** "Influence of Exercise Intensity for Improving Depressed Mood in Depression: A Dose-Response Study." Behav Ther 47(4):527-537. doi:10.1016/j.beth.2016.04.003 (abstract).
- n: 24 women with MDD, 30-minute cycling sessions at three intensities or quiet rest.
- Finding: "Exercise reduced depressed mood 10 and 30 minutes following exercise, but this effect was not influenced by exercise intensity."
- Limits: 24 people, 30 minutes on a bike, mood for half an hour.

**What we may say.** Mindfulness-based therapy lowers relapse and walking lowers depression, as courses and programmes over weeks. One bout of exercise lifts mood for a while. A 3-minute body scan or a walk prompt is a small piece of those, and nothing shows the piece works alone.

## 7. Learning which session helps this person

**Schwerdtfeger, Tatschl, Rominger 2025.** "Effectiveness of 2 Just-in-Time Adaptive Interventions for Reducing Stress and Stabilizing Cardiac Autonomic Function: Microrandomized Trials." J Med Internet Res 27:e69582. doi:10.2196/69582 (full text, PMC12371293). The closest study to Vire's loop.
- Design and n: two preregistered micro-randomised trials, 60 and 50 young adults (mostly students). A wearable spotted moments of low HRV and triggered a 1-minute exercise: study 1, "0.1 Hz slow-paced breathing"; study 2, mindful breathing or "focusing attention on a nonliving object", randomised within each person.
- Finding: stress and rumination fell after every exercise "irrespective of the kind of prompt and intervention" (study 1 stress b = -0.12, p < .001). HRV rose more during the breathing when the prompt came at a low-HRV moment (b = 0.08, p = .02), and stayed higher for 10 minutes.
- **The catch:** for stress, mindful breathing was no better than looking at an object ("independent of the type of intervention"); the authors "cannot rule out ... mere order effects or psychological adaptation to the repeated assessment".
- Limits: healthy students, 4 days.

**Rauschenberg, Schirmbeck, Fechtelpeter et al. 2026.** "A machine learning-based ecological momentary intervention for mental health promotion in youth: a micro-randomized trial." Transl Psychiatry 16:487. doi:10.1038/s41398-026-04475-8 (abstract; full text PMC13615972). The one trial we found that set learned choice against random choice.
- n: 49 young people, up to 210 decision points each, randomised between machine-learning choice and random choice of exercise.
- Finding: "An initial, outcome-specific signal favoring ML-based over random assignment was observed for momentary resilience (B = 0.147, 95% CI 0.004 - 0.290, p = 0.044), whereas there was no evidence of beneficial effects on positive or negative affect."
- Limits: one of three outcomes, the interval nearly touches zero; the authors call for "larger, adequately powered MRTs".

**Klasnja, Smith, Seewald et al. 2019 (HeartSteps).** "Efficacy of Contextually Tailored Suggestions for Physical Activity: A Micro-randomized Optimization Trial of HeartSteps." Ann Behav Med 53(6):573-582. doi:10.1093/abm/kay067 (abstract; full text PMC6401341, read through the PMC page).
- n: 44 adults, 6 weeks, up to 5 randomised walk prompts a day.
- Finding: a prompt raised steps in the next 30 minutes by 14% on average (35 steps, p = .06); "initially ... by 66% (167 steps; p < .01)", then the effect "diminished linearly over time at 2% per day" and was "no longer distinguishable from zero by the 28th day". The authors put it down to habituation; people said the prompts "became boring after 2–4 weeks".
- Limits: steps, not mood; healthy adults. Directly relevant to a daily walk prompt.

**Fiß, Berndt, Anickova, Vaessen, Kraiss 2026 (ALERT).** "Addressing mental health with daily-life ecological momentary interventions (ALERT) in people at increased risk for mental disorders: A micro-randomized trial." J Affect Disord 406:121661. doi:10.1016/j.jad.2026.121661 (abstract).
- n: 72 young adults at risk, 16 days, four kinds of short exercise, mood checked 30 minutes later.
- Finding: kinds differed: gratitude and savoring lowered negative affect; acceptance helped "during moments of higher NA"; "The cognitive reappraisal EMI neither improved NA nor PA."
- Limits: average effects by kind, not per person; no control for the longer-term change.

**Nahum-Shani and Murphy 2026.** "Just-in-Time Adaptive Interventions: Where Are We Now and What Is Next?" Annu Rev Psychol 77:679-703. doi:10.1146/annurev-psych-121024-044244 (abstract). Review; says people "may not be able to engage with ... an intervention when they need it most" and engagement is "generally suboptimal"; "much research effort is needed".

Also relevant: Vaschillo 2006 (section 3), the best breathing pace differs between people and stays stable over 10 sessions.

**What we may say.** "Each day Vire tries a session and records what happened to your breathing, your stillness and your next minute. That is how researchers test this, in micro-randomised trials. Whether picking per person beats picking at random is still open." `claims.md` "Removed" #23 still applies: after a low day the next reading tends to be higher anyway.

## 8. AirPods Pro 3 heart rate: what apps can read

**Apple, AirPods Pro 3 tech specs** (apple.com/airpods-pro/specs/, opened 14 Nov 2026).
- Sensors listed: "Dual beamforming microphones", "Inward-facing microphone", "Skin-detect sensor", "Motion-detecting accelerometer", "Speech-detecting accelerometer", "Heart rate sensor for workouts", "Touch control". No EEG or brain sensor.
- Footnote: the heart rate monitor is "intended for use during workouts with the Fitness app and compatible third-party apps on iPhone running iOS 26 and later".

**Apple Support 123184**, "Use heart rate sensing with AirPods" (opened 14 Nov 2026).
- "AirPods Pro 3 feature in-ear heart rate sensors that can help you track your heart rate during workouts"; they measure "continuously during your workout"; supported third-party workout apps on iPhone can show it after the person lets the app "read heart rate data, and to read and record workouts"; data shows in the Health app.

**Apple developer documentation, HealthKit** (JSON behind developer.apple.com/documentation/healthkit, opened 14 Nov 2026).
- `HKWorkoutSession` (iOS 17.0+): "Collecting heart rate data on iPhone or iPad requires pairing with an external heart rate sensor because these devices don't have one." "iPhone typically locks during workouts. For privacy reasons, health data usually isn't accessible while the device is locked."
- `HKWorkoutSession.init(healthStore:configuration:)` and `HKLiveWorkoutBuilder`: iOS 26.0+. So a third-party iPhone app runs its own workout session from iOS 26.
- `HKQuantityTypeIdentifier.heartRate`: count per time, discrete values; "Sample data may be condensed and/or coalesced by HealthKit."
- `heartRateVariabilitySDNN`: "The system automatically records samples on Apple Watch." `HKHeartbeatSeriesSample` and the SDNN page do not mention AirPods. We found no Apple documentation that AirPods give apps beat-to-beat intervals or HRV.

**Doherty et al. 2026** (`claims.md` row 75), checked again in full text (PMC13132241):
- 40 healthy adults (mean age 23.8), graded treadmill, Polar H10 reference, firmware 8B25, iOS 26.1. "Heart-rate data were recorded during structured workout sessions using Apple's Fitness and Health frameworks"; exported afterwards "using Health Auto Export (version 8.3.5) at the highest available sampling frequency"; compared in "non-overlapping 5-s epochs".
- At rest: 3,435 epochs, MAE 1.31 bpm, SD of differences 2.79 bpm. Overall limits of agreement -10.27 to 10.22.
- It is still the only AirPods heart rate validation we found (Europe PMC, 14 Nov).

**Check of `claims.md` row 75 and `qa.md` Q10.** Both hold. Two things to add or watch:
1. Q10 says the study got heart rate "by running a workout session". True, but it was Apple's own Fitness and Health recording, read back from Health afterwards with an export app, not a third-party app reading it live. Our iPhone app reading it live in its own session (iOS 26) is not what was validated.
2. For the breathing session, the study compared 5-second averages. At six breaths a minute a breath takes 10 seconds, so 5-second values give two points per breath. Nobody has shown that AirPods heart rate follows the swing within a breath. Say "heart rate from AirPods Pro 3 during the session", never "HRV from AirPods". The swing we can show comes from the phone camera (row 24d: 16 people, not paced).

**Also relevant.** In-ear PPG is disturbed by the head and face: **Montanari, Ferlini, Balaji, Mascolo, Kawsar 2023**, "EarSet: A Multi-Modal Dataset for Studying the Impact of Head and Facial Movements on In-Ear PPG Signals", Sci Data 10:850, doi:10.1038/s41597-023-02762-3 (abstract): "head movements and facial expressions cause skin and tissue displacements around and inside the ear" (30 people, 16 motions; a dataset, not an accuracy result). So heart rate during the session is best read while the person is still, the same condition as the breathing rate.

## Looked for and could not support

- **That one 2 to 3 minute session lowers depressive symptoms.** No trial found. The nearest (Schwerdtfeger 2025, 1 minute) found no difference from looking at an object for stress.
- **That breathing or HRVB helps after TMS, or alongside it.** Europe PMC search for biofeedback or slow breathing with TMS and depression (14 Nov) found nothing relevant.
- **That HRVB alone, without the rest of Meru's programme, lowers depression in a randomised trial.** Meru's only RCT tested the whole package against a waitlist.
- **Breathing rate from AirPods motion.** No published study (Europe PMC and arXiv, 14 Nov). Earbud studies used eSense at 50 Hz or Samsung earbuds with microphones; AirPods give apps about 25 Hz (row 74).
- **Earbud motion reading breathing at six a minute or slower.** Not tested in the earbud papers; methods built for normal breathing can miss it (Khan 2025; Röddiger's filter starts at six).
- **Breathing rate while walking or talking from earbud motion.** The literature says it fails (RespEar). So the walk prompt cannot be measured the same way.
- **HRV or beat-to-beat timing from AirPods for apps.** Not documented by Apple; not validated.
- **That sound adapting to the person beats a fixed pacer.** No comparison found. The adaptive-tone device evidence is from blood pressure trials, partly by its maker, and its effect disappears in independent and blinded trials.
- **Body scan on its own for depression.** No meta-analysis found.
- **That personalised choice of session improves depression.** None. One MRT (Rauschenberg 2026) found a small signal on one of three momentary outcomes.
- **Effect sizes from Laborde 2022 and a depression figure from Lehrer 2020.** Could not open the full texts; we quote only their abstracts.
- **Caldwell and Steffen 2018** (Int J Psychophysiol 131:96-101, doi:10.1016/j.ijpsycho.2018.01.001), an RCT of HRVB added to psychotherapy in MDD: the abstract gives no n and the full text was closed, so it is dropped.
- **Brain waves, EEG, "calms the brain".** Nothing from AirPods supports this; the EEG findings in Zaccaro 2018 used lab EEG caps.
